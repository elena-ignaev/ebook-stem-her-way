# GIRLS IN THE SCHOOL LAB

---

*Let's talk about something important before we begin.*

*For a lot of girls, the school laboratory can feel intimidating. The equipment looks complicated. The instructions sound technical. There's a pressure to get everything right on the first try, and sometimes — if you look around — you might feel like you're being watched more closely than your classmates, as if people are waiting to see whether you'll manage.*

*Here's what we want you to know: that feeling is common, it makes complete sense given the world we live in, and it is not an accurate reflection of whether you belong there.*

*Laboratory science is not a performance. It is an investigation. A good scientist is not someone who never makes mistakes — it is someone who notices mistakes carefully, understands what caused them, and uses that information. The lab is not a place where you prove you're smart. It is a place where you find things out.*

*The ten ideas in this part are designed to help you feel at home in the school lab — or to recreate school-lab experiences at home with simple equipment. They're organized around real scientific techniques: testing solutions with indicators, looking at cells under a microscope, separating mixtures, and studying living systems.*

*Some of these experiments you may have done in class already. If so, try them again with a scientist's eye — not just following the procedure, but asking: Why this step? What would happen if I changed this? What does this result mean?*

*The lab belongs to you.*

## Ideas 11–13: pH Testing, Onion Cells, Separating Mixtures

---

### IDEA 11 — TESTING pH: THE ACID-BASE SCALE IN EVERYDAY LIFE
**Using Natural Indicators to Test Common Liquids**

**Where:** Kitchen or school lab
**Time:** 40–60 minutes
**Mess level:** Medium — use old clothes

---

**Background: What Is pH?**

pH is a scale that measures how acidic or basic (alkaline) a solution is. It runs from 0 to 14:
- **0–6:** Acidic (the closer to 0, the more acidic)
- **7:** Neutral (pure water)
- **8–14:** Basic/Alkaline (the closer to 14, the more basic)

The "p" stands for "potenz" (German for "power") and the "H" stands for hydrogen. The pH scale is a measure of the concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺) in a solution.

pH matters everywhere: in the ocean, in your blood, in soil, in food preservation, in medicine, and in industrial chemistry.

---

**What You Need:**

*For the natural indicator (red cabbage indicator):*
- Half a head of red/purple cabbage
- Water
- A pot, a stove or kettle (ask an adult)
- A strainer

*For testing:*
- Small clear cups or test tubes
- Liquids to test: lemon juice, vinegar, baking soda solution, milk, tap water, orange juice, sparkling water, soap solution, tea

---

**Making the Indicator:**

1. Chop the red cabbage into small pieces.
2. Place in a pot with enough water to cover.
3. Bring to a boil, then simmer for 10 minutes. The water will turn deep purple.
4. Strain out the cabbage pieces. Let the purple liquid cool.
5. This is your indicator! Red cabbage contains a pigment called *anthocyanin* that changes color in response to pH.

---

**What To Do:**

1. Pour a small amount of the purple cabbage indicator into each cup.
2. Add a small amount of each test liquid to a different cup.
3. Stir and observe the color change.

**Color Guide:**
- Red / Pink: Very acidic (pH 1–3)
- Red-purple: Acidic (pH 4–5)
- Purple (unchanged): Neutral (pH 7)
- Blue-green: Mildly basic (pH 8–10)
- Yellow-green: Very basic (pH 11–14)

---

**Record Your Results:**

Make a table:

| Liquid | Color Change | Estimated pH | Acidic or Basic? |
|--------|-------------|-------------|-----------------|
| Lemon juice | | | |
| Vinegar | | | |
| Baking soda water | | | |
| Milk | | | |
| Tap water | | | |

---

**What's Happening?**

Anthocyanin (the pigment in red cabbage) is a *pH indicator* — a molecule that changes structure slightly depending on the concentration of hydrogen ions in its surroundings. This structural change affects how the molecule absorbs light, which changes the color we see.

Commercial pH strips work on the same principle — they're impregnated with synthetic indicators that change color predictably at different pH levels.

Your body is extremely careful about pH. Blood must be maintained between pH 7.35 and 7.45 — a range so narrow that a deviation of even 0.1 can cause serious health problems. Your body uses a system called *buffering* to maintain this range.

---

**Go Further:**
- Can you test pH of rainwater? Is it neutral, or slightly acidic? (Hint: CO₂ in the atmosphere dissolves in rain to form carbonic acid, giving clean rainwater a pH of about 5.6. Acid rain can have pH values as low as 4 or 3.)
- What is the pH of ocean water? How is *ocean acidification* (a consequence of climate change) affecting marine life?
- Make pH strips by dipping strips of coffee filter paper in your cabbage indicator solution and letting them dry. Use them to test substances later.

**What You Learned:**
*pH, acids, bases, indicators, anthocyanin, chemistry of everyday substances.*

### IDEA 12 — OBSERVING ONION CELLS UNDER A MICROSCOPE
**Your First Look at the Building Blocks of Life**

**Where:** School lab (microscope required; some toy microscopes work for this)
**Time:** 30–45 minutes
**Mess level:** Low

---

**What You Need:**
- A compound microscope (minimum 40x magnification)
- Microscope slides and coverslips
- A fresh onion
- Tweezers or a toothpick
- A dropper or pipette
- Water
- Iodine solution (optional, for staining — available at pharmacies)
- Scissors

---

**What To Do:**

1. Peel a dry outer layer from the onion and discard it. Break one of the inner, slightly moist layers and look for the thin, transparent membrane on the inner surface — this is the *epidermis*.

2. Carefully peel off a very small piece of this transparent membrane (1 cm × 1 cm or smaller). Use your tweezers.

3. Place the membrane flat onto the center of a clean microscope slide.

4. Add 1–2 drops of water (or iodine solution for better contrast). Iodine stains starch orange-brown and makes cell nuclei more visible.

5. Gently lower a coverslip over the specimen at an angle to avoid air bubbles.

6. Place the slide on the microscope stage and start with the lowest magnification.

7. Focus carefully, then switch to higher magnification.

---

**What to Look For:**

At 40x magnification or higher, you should be able to see:
- The rectangular outline of individual cells (onion cells have rigid cell walls that give them their geometric shape)
- The *cell wall* — the outermost boundary
- The *cell membrane* — just inside the cell wall
- The *nucleus* — a dark oval dot inside each cell (easier to see with iodine staining)
- The *vacuole* — a large central space inside each cell (takes up most of the cell's volume)

---

**What's Happening?**

Onion cells are plant cells, which means they have several features that animal cells (including human cells) don't have: a rigid *cell wall* made of cellulose (which is why they look like bricks), large central *vacuoles* that store water and waste, and *chloroplasts* — though not in this outer skin layer, which is why the cells are colorless.

The nucleus contains the cell's DNA — the complete genetic instructions for the entire onion plant. Every cell has a complete copy.

When Robert Hooke first looked at cork under a microscope in 1665, he described the tiny compartments he saw as "cells" — because they looked like the small rooms (cells) where monks lived. He had no idea he was looking at the fundamental unit of all life. You're now seeing the same thing he saw, with the same sense of wonder.

---

**Go Further:**
- Compare onion cells to other plant cells: try looking at the thin outer skin of a tomato, a leaf, or the cells of a piece of fruit.
- If you can get a prepared slide of human cheek cells, compare the animal cell to the plant cell. What's the same? What's different?
- Try making a wet mount of pond water. What living things can you find?

**What You Learned:**
*Cell structure, plant cells, cell wall, nucleus, vacuole, microscopy technique, the history of cell biology.*

### IDEA 13 — SEPARATING MIXTURES: FILTRATION, SEDIMENTATION, AND EVAPORATION
**How Scientists Get Pure Substances from Messy Mixtures**

**Where:** Kitchen or school lab
**Time:** 45–60 minutes for all three methods
**Mess level:** Low to medium

---

**Background:**

A *mixture* is a combination of two or more substances that are not chemically combined — they keep their individual properties and can, in principle, be separated. Separating mixtures is one of the most important skills in chemistry. Chemists separate mixtures to get pure substances, to analyze what's in a sample, or to process materials for industry or medicine.

There are many separation methods. You're going to explore three:

---

**Method A — Filtration (Removing Insoluble Solids)**

*Use when: one substance is solid and doesn't dissolve in the liquid.*

Materials: sand and water mixture, a funnel, coffee filter or filter paper, a beaker or jar.

1. Stir sand into water to make a murky mixture.
2. Fold a piece of coffee filter paper into a cone shape and fit it into the funnel.
3. Place the funnel over the jar.
4. Slowly pour the sand-water mixture through the filter.
5. The water passes through. The sand stays on the filter.

*Why it works:* Filter paper has tiny pores that allow small molecules (like water) to pass through, but trap larger solid particles (like sand).

---

**Method B — Sedimentation (Letting Gravity Work)**

*Use when: solid particles are heavy enough to settle over time.*

Materials: muddy water or a chalk powder and water mixture, a clear jar.

1. Shake the muddy water mixture vigorously.
2. Place it on a shelf and leave it completely undisturbed for 30 minutes to an hour.
3. Observe how the particles settle to the bottom, leaving clearer water above.

*Why it works:* Gravity pulls denser particles downward. Given time, they settle. This is called sedimentation. Pouring the clear top liquid carefully off is called *decanting*.

---

**Method C — Evaporation (Recovering Dissolved Solids)**

*Use when: you have a dissolved solid and want to recover it — like getting salt back from salt water.*

Materials: salt water solution, a shallow dish or tray.

1. Pour salty water into a wide, shallow dish.
2. Leave in a warm, sunny place (or gently heat on a stove with adult supervision).
3. Wait for the water to evaporate.
4. As the water disappears, you'll see salt crystals forming.

*Why it works:* Water molecules evaporate (turn into gas and escape into the air) when heated. The salt molecules, too heavy to evaporate, are left behind. This is also how sea salt is harvested in coastal saltpans around the world.

---

**Connecting to the Real World:**

- *Water treatment plants* use filtration, sedimentation, and chemical treatment to clean water.
- *Pharmaceutical companies* use sophisticated versions of these techniques to isolate pure drug compounds.
- *Mining operations* separate valuable minerals from rock using density differences and chemical extraction.
- *Coffee and tea* are prepared using filtration every day — the filter keeps the grounds back while the liquid passes through.

---

**Go Further:**
- Can you design a more effective sand filter? (Try layering sand, gravel, and charcoal in a bottle with holes poked in the bottom — this mimics a basic water filter.)
- What would you do if you needed to separate salt AND sand from water? (You'd need multiple steps. Think it through.)
- Research how communities without access to clean water use available materials to filter and purify water.

**What You Learned:**
*Filtration, sedimentation, evaporation, mixtures, separation techniques, real-world applications.*

### IDEA 14 — ACID-BASE REACTIONS: PRODUCING GAS AND NEUTRALIZATION
**Watching Acids and Bases React**

**Where:** Kitchen or school lab
**Time:** 30–40 minutes
**Mess level:** Medium

---

**What You Need:**
- Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) — a base
- White vinegar, lemon juice, or citric acid solution — acids
- Baking powder (a mixture of baking soda and a solid acid)
- Clear cups or test tubes
- Balloons
- Small plastic bottles
- Measuring spoons

---

**Experiment A — Gas Trapping:**

1. Add 2 tablespoons of baking soda to a small plastic bottle.
2. Stretch the neck of a balloon over a separate container.
3. Pour 3 tablespoons of vinegar into the balloon using a funnel.
4. Fit the balloon neck over the bottle opening, keeping the vinegar in the balloon.
5. When ready, tip the balloon upright so the vinegar falls into the bottle.
6. The balloon will inflate as CO₂ is produced!

*This demonstrates:* The acid-base reaction produces CO₂ gas, and you can collect and measure it. Baking uses this principle — baking powder in a cake produces CO₂ when it heats up, making the cake rise.

---

**Experiment B — Neutralization:**

Neutralization happens when an acid and a base react exactly to produce a neutral salt and water.

1. Make a very dilute baking soda solution (¼ teaspoon in 1 cup of water). This is your base.
2. Make a red cabbage indicator solution (from Idea 11).
3. Add 1 tablespoon of the baking soda solution to a cup and add indicator. Note the color (it should be blue-green: basic).
4. Very slowly add drops of vinegar, stirring after each. Watch the color change.
5. At the exact neutral point, the indicator will return to its original purple.
6. If you add too much vinegar, it will turn pink/red.

*The equation:*
NaHCO₃ (baking soda) + CH₃COOH (acetic acid/vinegar) → CH₃COONa (sodium acetate, a neutral salt) + H₂O + CO₂

You are watching the chemistry of *antacids* happen in real time. When someone has heartburn (too much stomach acid), they take an antacid — which is a base that neutralizes the excess acid in the stomach.

---

**What You Learned:**
*Acid-base chemistry, neutralization, gas production, real-world applications (antacids, baking, fermentation).*

### IDEA 15 — STARCH TESTING IN FOOD: THE IODINE TEST
**Identifying a Macronutrient**

**Where:** Kitchen or school lab
**Time:** 30 minutes
**Mess level:** Low (iodine stains — use old cloth)

---

**Background:**

Starch is a *polysaccharide* — a large carbohydrate molecule made of long chains of glucose. It's one of the most important energy storage molecules in plants. When you eat foods containing starch (bread, pasta, rice, potatoes), your digestive system breaks the starch back down into glucose, which your cells use for energy.

*Iodine solution* (iodine dissolved in potassium iodide) turns from amber-brown to dark blue-black in the presence of starch. This is because the iodine molecules fit inside the spiral structure of starch molecules, and this changes how they absorb light.

---

**What You Need:**
- Iodine solution (available at pharmacies — ask for iodine antiseptic)
- A dropper or pipette
- A white plate or white paper
- Foods to test: bread, cracker, potato, apple, onion, rice (cooked), pasta, cheese, egg white, sugar, flour

---

**What To Do:**

1. Place small samples of each food on your plate.
2. Add 2–3 drops of iodine solution to each sample.
3. Observe and record the color: Does it turn blue-black (starch present)? Or stay amber-brown (no starch)?

---

**Make a Prediction First:**

Before you test, predict which foods you think contain starch. Write it down. Then see how your predictions compare to the results.

*This is one of the most important habits in science: predicting before observing. A prediction based on prior knowledge and reasoning is called a hypothesis. Whether it's right or wrong, you learn from testing it.*

---

**Expected Results:**

High starch: bread, crackers, potato, cooked pasta, rice, flour
Low or no starch: apple, onion, cheese, egg white, sugar (note: sugar is a simple carbohydrate, not a polysaccharide — iodine does not react with it)

---

**Going Deeper:**

Saliva contains an enzyme called *amylase* that begins breaking down starch in your mouth. You can test this:

1. Chew a plain cracker for 2–3 minutes without swallowing.
2. Notice that it starts to taste slightly sweet — this is the starch being converted to sugar by your salivary amylase.
3. Spit a small amount onto a test surface and add iodine.
4. Compare to an un-chewed cracker. Is there less starch in the chewed sample?

**What You Learned:**
*Starch, polysaccharides, carbohydrates, iodine indicator, enzyme action, digestion, macronutrients.*

### IDEA 16 — INVESTIGATING HOW TEMPERATURE AFFECTS DISSOLVING SPEED
**A Controlled Experiment**

**Where:** Kitchen or school lab
**Time:** 40–50 minutes
**Mess level:** Low

---

**This is a proper controlled experiment — the kind scientists run every day.**

A controlled experiment tests one variable at a time while keeping everything else the same. This is how you can be confident that your results are caused by the thing you changed, and not by something else.

In this experiment, your variable is *temperature*. Everything else — the type of solute, the amount of solute, the size of the crystals, how you stir — should stay the same.

---

**Your Question:** Does temperature affect how quickly sugar (or salt) dissolves in water?

**Your Hypothesis:** Write down your prediction before you start.

**Variables:**
- *Independent variable* (what you change): temperature of water (cold, room temperature, hot)
- *Dependent variable* (what you measure): time taken for the sugar to dissolve
- *Controlled variables* (what stays the same): type and amount of sugar, amount of water, stirring rate, size of sugar crystals

---

**What You Need:**
- 3 identical cups or beakers
- Cold water, room temperature water, hot water (just boiled — ask an adult)
- A thermometer (recommended, to measure the exact temperature of each)
- 3 equal portions of granulated sugar (or salt): 2 teaspoons each
- A stopwatch or phone timer
- A spoon for stirring (stir the same number of times per second for each trial)

---

**What To Do:**

1. Measure and record the temperature of each water sample.
2. Pour equal amounts of each water temperature into separate cups.
3. At the same moment (or as close as possible), add one portion of sugar to each cup.
4. Stir each at the same rate. Start your timer.
5. Record how long it takes for the sugar to fully dissolve in each cup.
6. Repeat the experiment 2–3 times to make sure your results are consistent (this is called *replication*).

---

**Record Your Data:**

| Trial | Cold Water Temp | Time | Room Temp | Time | Hot Water Temp | Time |
|-------|----------------|------|-----------|------|---------------|------|
| 1 | | | | | | |
| 2 | | | | | | |
| 3 | | | | | | |
| Average | | | | | | |

---

**What's Happening?**

Dissolving happens when solvent molecules (water) break apart and surround solute molecules (sugar). At higher temperatures, the water molecules move faster (they have more kinetic energy). This means:
1. They collide more frequently with sugar molecules.
2. They break apart the sugar crystal structure more quickly.
3. They surround sugar molecules more effectively.

The result: hot water dissolves sugar significantly faster than cold water.

This has practical applications everywhere: hot tea dissolves sugar quickly; cold drinks need stirring much longer; industrial chemical processes often use heat to speed up dissolving.

---

**What You Learned:**
*Dissolving, solubility, temperature effects, experimental design, variables, data collection, replication.*

### IDEA 17 — THE BALLOON LUNG MODEL
**How Your Lungs Work**

**Where:** Home or school lab
**Time:** 30–40 minutes to build; use it for ongoing demos
**Mess level:** Low

---

**What You Need:**
- A clear plastic bottle (1–2 liters), with the bottom cut off
- 2 small balloons (for the lungs)
- 1 large balloon (cut in half crossways — for the diaphragm)
- 2 short straws (or a Y-shaped plastic connector)
- Tape and scissors
- Plasticine or modeling clay (to seal gaps)

---

**What To Do:**

1. Cut the bottom off the plastic bottle. This is your chest cavity.

2. Connect the two small balloons to the two ends of a Y-shaped straw or two straws joined together. These represent the lungs connected to the windpipe (trachea).

3. Insert the straw end up through the bottle cap and seal around it with clay. The small balloons should hang inside the bottle.

4. Take the large balloon, cut off the neck, and stretch the remaining dome over the open bottom of the bottle. Secure with tape or a rubber band. This represents the diaphragm.

5. Try pulling down on the bottom balloon (the diaphragm) while watching the small balloons inside. They should inflate.

6. Push the bottom balloon up. The small balloons deflate.

---

**What's Happening?**

Your model demonstrates the mechanics of breathing:

When you breathe in (*inhalation*):
- Your diaphragm (a dome-shaped muscle below your lungs) contracts and flattens, pulling downward.
- This increases the volume of the chest cavity.
- Increasing volume decreases pressure (Boyle's Law: pressure and volume are inversely related at constant temperature).
- Air rushes from higher pressure outside to lower pressure inside your lungs.

When you breathe out (*exhalation*):
- Your diaphragm relaxes and moves upward (dome shape again).
- This decreases chest cavity volume, increasing pressure.
- Air moves from higher pressure inside to lower pressure outside.

The model shows this beautifully: pulling the bottom balloon down represents the diaphragm flattening, which inflates the "lungs" inside. It's the exact same principle — pressure difference driving airflow.

**What You Learned:**
*Respiratory system, diaphragm, lung mechanics, Boyle's Law, pressure and volume.*

### IDEA 18 — MEASURING HEART RATE BEFORE AND AFTER EXERCISE
**Doing Real Exercise Physiology**

**Where:** Home or school (needs a bit of space to exercise)
**Time:** 30–40 minutes
**Mess level:** None

---

**What You Need:**
- A stopwatch or phone with a timer
- Your fingers and a pulse point (wrist or neck)
- A notebook and pen

---

**Finding Your Pulse:**

Place two fingers (index and middle) gently on the inside of your wrist, just below the base of your thumb. Or on the side of your neck, below the jaw. Don't press too hard — a light touch is enough.

Count the beats for 15 seconds. Multiply by 4. This gives you your heart rate in beats per minute (BPM).

---

**The Experiment:**

1. **Resting rate:** Sit quietly for 2 minutes, then measure your heart rate. This is your *resting heart rate*. Record it.

2. **After light exercise:** Do 2 minutes of slow walking in place. Measure your heart rate immediately. Record.

3. **After moderate exercise:** Do 2 minutes of brisk jogging in place or jumping jacks. Measure immediately. Record.

4. **After intense exercise:** Do 2 minutes of sprinting in place as fast as you can. Measure immediately. Record.

5. **Recovery:** Sit still and measure your heart rate every 2 minutes for 10 minutes. How long does it take to return to your resting rate?

---

**Your Data Table:**

| Condition | Heart Rate (BPM) |
|-----------|-----------------|
| Resting | |
| After light exercise | |
| After moderate exercise | |
| After intense exercise | |
| 2 min recovery | |
| 4 min recovery | |
| 6 min recovery | |
| 8 min recovery | |
| 10 min recovery | |

---

**What's Happening?**

Your heart pumps oxygenated blood to your muscles and organs. During exercise, your muscles need more oxygen to produce energy — so your heart beats faster to deliver more blood.

The amount of blood your heart pumps per minute is:
**Cardiac Output = Heart Rate × Stroke Volume**

(Stroke volume is the amount of blood pumped per beat — typically about 70 ml at rest.)

At rest: ~70 BPM × 70 ml = ~4,900 ml/min (about 5 liters)
During intense exercise: up to 200 BPM × 120 ml = ~24,000 ml/min!

Recovery time (how long it takes your heart rate to return to normal) is an indicator of cardiovascular fitness. Athletes generally recover faster because their hearts are stronger and more efficient.

---

**Go Further:**
- Compare resting heart rates among classmates or family members. Are there differences? What factors might influence resting heart rate?
- What is maximum heart rate? How is it estimated? (Common formula: 220 − your age)
- Research: why do trained athletes sometimes have resting heart rates as low as 40–50 BPM, while the average is 60–100?

**What You Learned:**
*Heart rate, cardiovascular physiology, exercise and the body, cardiac output, recovery rate, experimental data collection.*

### IDEA 19 — MODELING THE HUMAN CIRCULATORY SYSTEM
**Mapping the Body's Transport Network**

**Where:** Desk or table
**Time:** 45–60 minutes
**Mess level:** Low

---

**What You Need:**
- A large sheet of poster paper or flip chart paper
- Red and blue colored pens or markers
- A basic diagram of the human body outline (you can trace your own or print one)
- Reference: a biology textbook or trusted internet image of the circulatory system

---

**What To Do:**

1. Draw (or trace) a simple outline of the human body on your poster paper.

2. Draw the heart in the center of the chest — slightly to the left.

3. Using *red* for oxygenated blood and *blue* for deoxygenated blood, draw the major blood vessels:
   - The *aorta* (main artery leaving the heart, carrying oxygenated blood to the body)
   - The *pulmonary artery* (carrying deoxygenated blood from the heart to the lungs)
   - The *pulmonary veins* (carrying oxygenated blood from the lungs back to the heart)
   - The *vena cava* (carrying deoxygenated blood from the body back to the heart)
   - Major arteries to the head, arms, and legs
   - Major veins returning from the head, arms, and legs

4. Label all the structures.

5. Draw arrows to show the direction of blood flow.

6. In a corner of the poster, draw a simple diagram of the *four chambers* of the heart:
   - Right atrium and right ventricle (receive and pump deoxygenated blood)
   - Left atrium and left ventricle (receive and pump oxygenated blood)

---

**Understanding the Two Loops:**

The circulatory system is actually two connected loops:

**Pulmonary circulation:** Heart → lungs → heart
Deoxygenated blood leaves the right ventricle, travels to the lungs (where it picks up oxygen and releases CO₂), then returns to the left atrium.

**Systemic circulation:** Heart → body → heart
Oxygenated blood leaves the left ventricle through the aorta, travels to all organs and tissues (delivering oxygen, picking up CO₂), then returns to the right atrium.

---

**The Heart as a Pump:**

The heart is a *double pump*. The right side pumps blood to the lungs; the left side pumps blood to the rest of the body. The heart has four chambers (atria on top, ventricles on the bottom) and four valves that ensure blood flows in only one direction.

In an average lifetime, the human heart beats approximately *2.5 billion times* without stopping.

**What You Learned:**
*Circulatory system, heart anatomy, arteries and veins, oxygenated/deoxygenated blood, pulmonary and systemic circulation.*

### IDEA 20 — INVESTIGATING HOW LIGHT AFFECTS PLANT GROWTH
**A Multi-Week Biology Experiment**

**Where:** Home — needs a sunny and a dark/shaded spot
**Time:** Setup 20 minutes; observations over 2–4 weeks
**Mess level:** Very low

---

**This is a long-term experiment. The setup takes minutes, but the real science happens over weeks as you observe and record changes.**

---

**What You Need:**
- 6–8 identical small pots or yogurt cups with holes poked in the bottom
- Potting soil
- Seeds of the same type (beans, cress, sunflowers, or lettuce work well — fast-growing ones are best)
- Water
- Labels
- A notebook for daily observations

---

**Setting Up:**

1. Fill all pots with the same amount of soil.
2. Plant the same number of seeds (3–4 per pot) at the same depth in all pots.
3. Water all pots equally.
4. Place the pots in three different light conditions:
   - **Group A (Full light):** Sunny windowsill or outdoors
   - **Group B (Partial light):** Indoors away from the window, some indirect light
   - **Group C (No light):** Inside a cupboard or box — completely dark

5. Water all pots equally every 2 days. Keep all other conditions (temperature, soil, water amount) as identical as possible.

---

**Observe and Record Daily:**

For each group, record:
- Date
- Height of seedlings (in cm)
- Color of stems and leaves (green, pale yellow, white?)
- General health (strong and upright? Thin and droopy?)
- Any other observations (roots showing? Leaves curling?)

Draw simple sketches of each group's plants every few days.

---

**What to Expect:**

*Full light plants:* Will be green, compact, sturdy, healthy.
*Partial light plants:* Will grow somewhat normally, but may be slightly paler and taller (reaching for light).
*No light plants:* Will sprout (they have stored energy in the seed) but will be *etiolated* — pale yellow or white, very tall and thin, with small or no leaves. They will eventually die without light.

---

**What's Happening?**

Plants make their own food through *photosynthesis* — using light energy, water, and carbon dioxide to produce glucose and oxygen. The green color of plants comes from *chlorophyll*, the pigment that absorbs light.

Without light, plants cannot photosynthesize. They use the stored energy in the seed at first (this is why dark-grown plants can still sprout), but once that's used up, there is no energy source for growth.

Etiolation (the pale, stretched growth in darkness) is actually an adaptation — the plant is literally stretching upward as fast as possible, trying to reach light. This is a programmed response to being in darkness, controlled by plant hormones.

**What You Learned:**
*Photosynthesis, chlorophyll, plant growth, light as an energy source, etiolation, controlled experiments, long-term data collection.*

# ENGINEERING AND DESIGN FOR EVERYDAY LIFE

---

*Engineering is not just about machines and factories. It is the art of solving problems — using creativity, materials, and systematic thinking to build things that work.*

*Every object in your life is the product of engineering: the chair you sit on, the bag you carry to school, the bridge you cross on the way to the market, the water that comes from your tap. Someone looked at a problem — "people need to cross this river," "water needs to get to people's homes," "students need something to carry their books" — and designed a solution.*

*The experiments in Part 3 invite you to think like an engineer. This means thinking not just about what you're building, but why you're building it, what constraints you're working under, and how you will test whether your design works.*

*Engineers use something called the Design Process:*

**1. Identify the problem.** What exactly are you trying to solve?
**2. Research.** What do you already know? What can you learn?
**3. Brainstorm solutions.** Think of as many ideas as possible without judging them.
**4. Choose a design.** Pick one idea and plan it out.
**5. Build it.** Make your design with the materials available.
**6. Test it.** Does it do what it's supposed to do?
**7. Evaluate and improve.** What worked? What didn't? How can you make it better?
**8. Share your results.** Communication is part of engineering.

These steps aren't always linear — you'll often loop back and redesign. That's not failure. That's engineering.

## Ideas 21–23: Wooden Bridge, Paper Tower, Balloon Car

### IDEA 21 — BUILDING A BRIDGE FROM POPSICLE STICKS
**Structural Engineering in Miniature**

**Where:** Table or desk
**Time:** 1–2 hours
**Mess level:** Low (some glue)

---

**The Challenge:**

Design and build a bridge using only popsicle sticks and white glue that can:
- Span a gap of at least 30 cm (place it between two books or stacks)
- Hold as much weight as possible

---

**What You Need:**
- Popsicle sticks / craft sticks (50–100)
- White glue or craft glue
- Binder clips or clothes pegs (to hold pieces while glue dries)
- 2 books or boxes to serve as bridge supports
- Coins or small weights for testing
- A ruler

---

**Engineering Concepts to Think About:**

Before you build, learn about the forces at work:

*Compression:* Forces that push or squeeze. When you stand on a bridge, gravity pushes down — the bridge deck is under compression.

*Tension:* Forces that pull or stretch. In a suspension bridge, the cables are under tension — they're being stretched.

*Triangles are strong!* If you look at bridges, trusses, and towers, you'll see triangles everywhere. A triangle is the only polygon that cannot be deformed without changing the length of its sides. Squares and rectangles can be pushed into parallelograms — triangles cannot. This makes them the engineer's best friend.

---

**Design Phase:**

Sketch your bridge before building. Consider:
- How will you distribute weight across the bridge?
- Will you use a truss design? (Rows of triangles.)
- How will the bridge connect to its supports?
- How can you strengthen the roadway (flat surface)?

Look at real bridge designs online for inspiration: beam bridges, arch bridges, truss bridges, suspension bridges. Each handles forces differently.

---

**Building Phase:**

Build your bridge according to your design, letting glue dry fully at each stage (or use binder clips to hold joints while they dry — this takes patience, but it matters!).

*Tip:* A bridge built with triangular trusses on its sides will outperform a simple flat bridge of the same materials.

---

**Testing Phase:**

Place the bridge across the gap. Load coins or weights onto the center (the weakest point). Record how much weight it holds before it fails. Observe where it fails first — this tells you where your design was weakest.

---

**Go Further:**
- Research the Forth Bridge in Scotland or the Millau Viaduct in France — these are engineering marvels. What structural principles do they use?
- Can you redesign your bridge after seeing where it failed?

**What You Learned:**
*Structural engineering, compression and tension, triangles in structures, design process, bridge types.*

### IDEA 22 — DESIGNING A TOWER FROM PAPER THAT HOLDS WEIGHT
**The Challenge of Vertical Load**

**Where:** Table or desk
**Time:** 30–45 minutes
**Mess level:** Low

---

**The Challenge:**

Using only *10 sheets of regular A4 paper* and *30 cm of tape*, build the tallest tower you can that can support the weight of a hardcover book on top for at least 10 seconds.

---

**Rules:**
- Only 10 sheets of paper (you can fold, roll, cut, curl, or layer them)
- Maximum 30 cm of tape total
- Must be freestanding (no leaning against walls)
- Must support a book on top for 10 seconds

---

**Think Before You Build:**

Paper seems flimsy — but it can be remarkably strong when used correctly. A flat sheet collapses easily under vertical load. But roll that same sheet into a cylinder, and it can support many times its own weight. This is because the rolled shape distributes force evenly around the circumference.

Consider:
- Columns (rolled paper tubes) can bear vertical loads
- A wide base gives stability
- Cross-bracing between columns prevents toppling
- A flat paper platform on top gives the book a stable resting surface

---

**Build, Test, Revise:**

Build your design, then test it. If it falls, look at where and why. Redesign. Try again. In competitive engineering challenges, teams often build multiple versions — and the later versions are almost always better.

---

**Real-World Connection:**

Tall buildings use similar principles: a strong, wide base; columns to carry load; cross-bracing or triangulated frames to resist sideways forces (like wind). The Eiffel Tower, for example, was designed as a lattice structure specifically to resist the wind that would have toppled a solid column of the same height.

**What You Learned:**
*Structural load, compression, columns, design iteration, engineering under constraints.*

### IDEA 23 — THE BALLOON-POWERED CAR
**Newton's Third Law in Motion**

**Where:** Smooth floor (hardwood or tile works best)
**Time:** 45–60 minutes to build and test
**Mess level:** Low

---

**The Challenge:**

Build a vehicle that moves using only the power of a balloon. The car that travels the farthest wins.

---

**What You Need:**
- A round balloon (longer sausage-shaped balloons actually work better)
- A flexible straw (or two straws taped together)
- A base: a piece of cardboard, an old plastic bottle, a small wooden board
- Wheels: bottle caps, spools, slices of cardboard tubes, small plastic wheels from a toy kit
- Skewers or pencils (as axles)
- Tape, scissors, push-pins or thumbtacks
- A smooth floor for testing

---

**The Science:**

Newton's Third Law of Motion: *For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.*

When you blow up the balloon and release it, air rushes out of the nozzle backward. The reaction to this force propels the car forward. This is the same principle that powers jet engines and rockets.

For your car to travel far, it needs:
- *Low friction:* Wheels that spin freely; a smooth floor; minimal weight
- *Efficient thrust:* A straight nozzle so air exits in one direction; a larger balloon for more air
- *Stability:* Balanced weight distribution so the car doesn't spin

---

**Building Tips:**
1. Thread your straw through a small hole in the balloon neck so air can be blown in through the straw.
2. Attach the straw (horizontally) along the underside or top of your vehicle base, pointing toward the back.
3. Attach your axles (skewers) through the base, with wheels at each end.
4. Make sure wheels are aligned and spin freely.
5. Blow up the balloon through the straw, pinch the straw shut, set the car down, and release.

---

**Test and Improve:**

- Does the car go straight? (If not, the thrust nozzle may not be aligned)
- How far does it travel?
- What changes improve the distance?

**What You Learned:**
*Newton's Third Law, thrust and propulsion, friction, engineering design, wheel and axle systems.*

### IDEA 24 — THE MINI WINDMILL
**Capturing Wind Energy**

**Where:** Outdoors or near a fan indoors
**Time:** 45–60 minutes
**Mess level:** Low

---

**The Challenge:**

Build a small windmill (wind turbine) that spins efficiently in a breeze and can lift a small object (like a paper clip on a thread) as it spins.

---

**What You Need:**
- A cork, styrofoam ball, or small ball of clay (for the hub)
- Popsicle sticks or stiff cardboard (for blades)
- A wooden skewer or pencil (as the axle)
- Thread or thin string
- A small paper clip or eraser (to lift)
- Tape and scissors
- A fan (to test indoors)

---

**Design Considerations:**

The angle of the blades matters enormously. Windmill blades are not flat — they are angled (like a propeller) so that when wind hits them, they produce rotational force. The angle of attack — the angle at which the blade meets the wind — determines efficiency.

Try different blade angles and observe which makes the windmill spin faster. Also consider the number of blades (most wind turbines have 3) and blade shape.

---

**The Science:**

Wind turbines convert *kinetic energy* (energy of moving air) into *rotational mechanical energy* (spinning motion) and ultimately into *electrical energy* when a generator is attached.

The amount of energy available in wind increases with the *cube* of the wind speed. This means doubling the wind speed gives you 8 times more energy. This is why offshore wind farms (where winds are consistently strong) produce much more energy than inland ones.

Wind energy is one of the fastest-growing renewable energy sources in the world. In many countries, it now generates electricity at lower cost than coal or gas.

**What You Learned:**
*Kinetic energy, wind turbine principles, blade design, angle of attack, renewable energy.*

### IDEA 25 — THE SIMPLE WATER FILTER
**Designing for Clean Water**

**Where:** Outdoors or kitchen sink (messy water involved)
**Time:** 45–60 minutes to build; testing takes 20 more
**Mess level:** Medium

---

**The Challenge:**

Design and build a multi-layer water filter using natural materials that can take visibly muddy water and produce clearer, cleaner water.

*Important note: Your filter will NOT produce water that is safe to drink. This is about understanding filtration principles — not about creating drinkable water, which requires additional disinfection (boiling, UV treatment, chemical treatment).*

---

**What You Need:**
- A large plastic bottle, cut in half
- Gravel or small pebbles
- Coarse sand
- Fine sand
- Activated charcoal (fish tank charcoal, from a pet store)
- Cotton balls or a piece of cloth
- A second container to collect filtered water
- Muddy water (mix soil into water)

---

**Layering Your Filter (from top to bottom in the upturned bottle half):**

1. Cotton ball or cloth (first layer — catches large debris)
2. Fine sand (5 cm)
3. Coarse sand (5 cm)
4. Gravel (5 cm)
5. Activated charcoal (3–4 cm) — this removes some chemical impurities and odors

Pour muddy water in at the top and collect the filtered water in a container below.

---

**The Science:**

Real water treatment uses the same principles, scaled up enormously:
1. *Screening* — removes large debris
2. *Sedimentation* — allows particles to settle
3. *Filtration* — passes water through sand and gravel
4. *Activated carbon filtration* — removes organic chemicals, chlorine, some metals
5. *Disinfection* — kills bacteria and viruses (using chlorine, UV, or ozone)

Access to clean water is one of the greatest global challenges. Over 2 billion people lack access to safe drinking water. Engineers, chemists, and public health scientists work together to solve this problem.

**What You Learned:**
*Water filtration, multi-stage treatment, activated charcoal, real-world water systems, global water challenges.*

### IDEA 26 — DESIGNING A HEAT-RESISTANT HOUSE FROM RECYCLED MATERIALS
**Thermal Insulation Engineering**

**Where:** Home
**Time:** 1–2 hours to build; testing takes 30 minutes
**Mess level:** Low to medium

---

**The Challenge:**

Using only recycled and everyday materials, build a small model house that keeps its interior as cool as possible when placed under a lamp or in direct sunlight. The house with the smallest temperature rise inside wins.

---

**What You Need:**
- Recycled cardboard boxes (various sizes)
- Newspaper, bubble wrap, aluminum foil, cotton fabric, styrofoam pieces
- Scissors, tape, glue
- 2 thermometers (or 1 that you alternate)
- A lamp or a sunny window

---

**Design Principles to Consider:**

*Color matters:* Light colors reflect heat; dark colors absorb it. White roofs stay cooler.

*Insulation:* Trapping air in small pockets slows heat transfer. Bubble wrap, crumpled newspaper, and styrofoam all work by this principle.

*Reflectivity:* Aluminum foil reflects radiant heat.

*Air circulation:* Good ventilation (small openings in the right places) allows hot air to escape and cooler air to enter.

---

**Test Your Design:**

1. Place your house model under a lamp or in sun.
2. Record the starting temperature inside.
3. After 15 minutes, record again. How much has it risen?
4. Compare to an uninsulated cardboard box as a control.

---

**Real-World Connection:**

In many parts of the world, cooling buildings is one of the largest uses of energy — and as climate change brings higher temperatures, this is only increasing. Passive cooling (using design rather than air conditioning) is a growing field in sustainable architecture. Traditional buildings in many cultures already incorporate these principles: thick mud walls in the Middle East, elevated houses in Southeast Asia, courtyard designs that channel air — these are all forms of passive thermal design developed over centuries.

**What You Learned:**
*Thermal insulation, heat transfer (conduction, convection, radiation), passive cooling, sustainable design, materials science.*

### IDEA 27 — THE SUSPENSION BRIDGE MODEL
**Engineering Under Tension**

**Where:** Table
**Time:** 60–90 minutes
**Mess level:** Low

---

**The Challenge:** Build a model suspension bridge using string, popsicle sticks, and tape that can span 40 cm and hold weight.

**Key Concept:** In a suspension bridge, the roadway hangs from cables (suspended). The cables are under *tension* — they're being pulled. The towers are under *compression* — they're being squashed downward by the cable's pull. By hanging the roadway, you distribute the weight across the long cables, which allows suspension bridges to span much longer distances than beam bridges.

Famous examples: the Golden Gate Bridge (San Francisco), the Forth Road Bridge (Scotland), the Jiaxing-Shaoxing Sea Bridge (China).

**Build It:**
1. Build two towers from popsicle sticks (layered and glued) — approximately 15 cm tall.
2. Run two main cable strings from one tower to the other, looping over the tops and anchoring at both ends.
3. Hang vertical "suspender" strings down from the main cable at regular intervals.
4. Attach a roadway of popsicle sticks to the bottom of the suspenders.
5. Test with increasing weight.

**What You Learned:** *Tension, compression, load distribution, suspension bridge design, long-span engineering.*

### IDEA 28 — THE MINI CATAPULT
**Projectile Motion and Simple Machines**

**Where:** Outdoors or large indoor space
**Time:** 45–60 minutes
**Mess level:** Low

---

**The Challenge:** Build a working catapult from popsicle sticks, a rubber band, and a spoon that launches a marshmallow as far as possible.

**What You Need:**
- 7–10 popsicle sticks
- Rubber bands
- A plastic spoon
- Marshmallows or small balls of paper as projectiles

**Build It:**
1. Stack 5 popsicle sticks and bind them tightly together with rubber bands at both ends — this is your base platform.
2. Take 2 more sticks and stand them vertically, one on each side of the platform, about one-third from one end. Bind them to the platform.
3. Lay one more stick across the two vertical ones (the launch arm pivot point) and secure loosely with a rubber band so it can rotate.
4. Attach the plastic spoon to one end of the launch arm. Connect a rubber band from the other end to the base platform (to create tension).
5. Load a marshmallow in the spoon, pull down and release.

**The Science:**

A catapult stores *potential energy* (in the stretched rubber band) and converts it to *kinetic energy* (projectile motion). Once launched, the marshmallow follows a curved path called a *parabola* — determined by its initial speed, launch angle, and gravity.

Projectile motion equations describe this path precisely. The optimal angle for maximum distance is 45°.

**What You Learned:** *Potential and kinetic energy, projectile motion, parabola, simple machines, lever mechanics.*

### IDEA 29 — THE SIMPLE PULLEY MACHINE
**Mechanical Advantage**

**Where:** Home or school
**Time:** 30–45 minutes
**Mess level:** Very low

---

**The Challenge:** Build a simple pulley system and demonstrate how it reduces the effort needed to lift a load.

**What You Need:**
- A spool of thread or a small wheel with a groove (or use a metal binder ring)
- String or thin rope
- A hook or pencil to mount the pulley from
- Weights (books, cans, bags of rice)
- A spring scale or kitchen scale (to measure force)

**Build It:**

Single fixed pulley:
1. Hang your spool/ring from a hook or over a door edge.
2. Run string over the groove.
3. Attach a weight to one end.
4. Pull down on the other end to lift the weight.

*With a fixed pulley, the effort equals the load — but the direction of force changes (you pull down to lift up). This is useful when pulling upward is awkward.*

Movable pulley (mechanical advantage = 2):
1. Attach one end of the string to the fixed point.
2. Run string around a movable pulley attached to the weight.
3. Pull the free end of the string.

*Now you only need half the force to lift the weight — but you have to pull twice the distance.*

This is *mechanical advantage* — a machine lets you trade force for distance (or distance for force).

**What You Learned:** *Simple machines, pulley, mechanical advantage, force and distance trade-off, work.*

### IDEA 30 — THE EGG DROP CHALLENGE
**Protecting a Fragile Cargo**

**Where:** Outdoors (or indoors with a high ceiling and forgiving floors)
**Time:** 60–90 minutes to design and build; 10 minutes to test
**Mess level:** Potentially very high — have paper towels ready

---

**The Ultimate Engineering Challenge:**

Design a device using only the allowed materials that will protect a raw egg from breaking when dropped from a height of at least 2 meters.

**Allowed Materials (pick a budget, e.g., only 20 items total from this list):**
- Straws, rubber bands, tape, cotton balls, bubble wrap, plastic bags, newspaper, cardboard, popsicle sticks, string, sponge pieces, foam, small plastic bags

**Rules:**
- The egg must be raw
- The device must be dropped (not thrown)
- The drop height is at least 2 meters (measure carefully)
- If the egg survives, increase the height

---

**Design Thinking:**

The physics challenge: When the device hits the ground, it decelerates rapidly. The egg inside experiences force (F = ma — the greater the deceleration, the greater the force). Your job is to *extend the time of deceleration* to reduce the peak force on the egg.

Strategies:
- *Crumple zone:* Soft, compressible material around the egg absorbs impact by deforming slowly.
- *Suspension:* Suspending the egg inside the device so it doesn't hit the walls directly on impact.
- *Drag:* A parachute slows descent, reducing impact speed.
- *Distribute impact:* Multiple contact points spread the force.

The best solutions often combine multiple strategies. Real-world equivalent: car safety engineering (crumple zones, airbags, seat belts all serve similar functions as your egg protection device).

After testing, examine failures. Where did the egg crack? What does that tell you about where your design was weak?

**What You Learned:** *Force, deceleration, impulse-momentum theorem, crumple zones, engineering design iteration, creative problem-solving.*
