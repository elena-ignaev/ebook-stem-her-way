# AUTHOR'S NOTE

---

Dear Reader,

This book was born from a simple observation — and a stubborn hope.

The observation: in classrooms around the world, girls often fall quiet when STEM topics come up. Not because they aren't curious. Not because they aren't capable. But because, somewhere along the way, too many of them received a quiet, damaging message: *This is not for you.*

The hope: that message is wrong. Completely, entirely, demonstrably wrong.

STEM — Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics — is one of the most human things we do. It is the act of looking at the world and asking, *Why does this work the way it does? Could it work better? What happens if I try this?* Those are not the questions of one gender or one type of person. They are the questions of every child who has ever stared at a caterpillar, wondered why the sky turns orange at sunset, or tried to figure out how a phone knows where you are.

You were born curious. This book is for that curiosity.

When we began compiling the 50 ideas in this book, we had one rule: every experiment, every project, every activity had to be accessible. Not every girl has a fully equipped school lab at home. Not every girl has a mentor who looks like her, or parents who work in science. Not every girl has had someone look at her and say, *You would be an incredible scientist.*

So this book tries to say that — and then show you exactly where to begin.

We also want to be honest with you about something: STEM is not always easy. It involves confusion, failed experiments, results that don't make sense, equations that take three tries to understand, and projects that fall apart the first time you build them. This is not a sign that you're failing. This is literally what science looks like from the inside. Every researcher you've ever admired has a drawer full of results that didn't work. Every engineer has a design that collapsed. Every mathematician has a proof they had to scrap and start over.

The difference between someone who succeeds in STEM and someone who gives up is rarely talent. It is almost always *persistence* — the ability to say, *Okay, that didn't work. Let me try again.*

This book does not require you to be good at science yet. It does not require you to have passed any particular test, to have scored high on any exam, or to have impressed any teacher. It only requires that you are at least a little bit curious, and that you are willing to try something — even if you're not sure it will work.

You belong in STEM. Not someday. Right now.

The experiments in this book can be done at home, at school, with friends, or alone. Some take ten minutes. Some might take a weekend. Some will make a mess (put down newspaper first). Some will surprise you. All of them will teach you something — not just about science, but about the most important STEM tool of all: your own mind.

We wrote this for you.

Now go make something.

*With so much encouragement,*

*The STEM for Girls Editorial Team*

# WHY STEM MATTERS FOR GIRLS

## Why STEM? Why Now? Why You?

---

### Block 1 — Why STEM Matters

Let's start with a number: by 2030, it is estimated that more than 75% of the fastest-growing careers in the world will require skills in science, mathematics, technology, or engineering. These are the jobs that will build the hospitals of the future, design the clean energy systems that power our cities, write the software that connects billions of people, and develop the medicines that will treat diseases we haven't even named yet.

These are good jobs — meaningful, creative, and well-paid. And right now, women hold fewer than 30% of them.

That is not because women and girls are less capable. Decades of research have shown that girls perform equally — and often better — than boys in math and science during their school years. The gap is not in ability. The gap is in encouragement, representation, and opportunity.

This matters beyond careers, too. STEM teaches ways of thinking that are useful everywhere:

**Critical thinking.** STEM teaches you to ask, *Is this claim actually supported by evidence?* This skill protects you from misinformation, helps you make better decisions, and makes you a clearer communicator.

**Problem-solving.** In STEM, you learn to break a big, impossible-seeming problem into small, manageable steps. This is not just useful in a laboratory — it's useful in every challenge life throws at you.

**Creativity.** The best scientists are deeply creative people. Designing an experiment, figuring out why something isn't working, or imagining a new way to use existing materials all require imagination. STEM and creativity are not opposites — they are partners.

**Resilience.** Perhaps most importantly: STEM teaches you how to fail well. Every hypothesis that gets disproven, every bridge model that collapses, every line of code that throws an error — these are not failures. They are data. They tell you what to try next.

When more girls enter STEM, the whole world benefits. Research consistently shows that diverse teams produce better science. When women are at the table, different questions get asked — questions that lead to discoveries and solutions that might otherwise be missed. The COVID-19 vaccine was developed at record speed partly because of the contributions of Dr. Katalin Karikó, a woman who spent decades working on mRNA technology while many doubted her. The first images of a black hole were made possible by the algorithms of Dr. Katie Bouman, who developed the imaging methods as a graduate student. When girls step into STEM, history gets made.

---

### Block 2 — STEM Is Not "Just for Boys"

We need to say this clearly, because many girls have received the opposite message from school, from media, from toy stores, from the internet, and sometimes — unintentionally — from the people around them:

**STEM has never been only for boys.**

The stereotype of the lone male scientist in a lab coat is not accurate history. It is a story constructed over time by excluding women from credit, from recognition, and from the institutions where science was practiced. When we look more carefully at the history of science, we find women everywhere — often working behind the scenes, often uncredited, and often doing work that was central to the most important discoveries of their time.

Rosalind Franklin's X-ray crystallography images were used — without her full knowledge or credit — to help Watson and Crick identify the double helix structure of DNA. Lise Meitner co-discovered nuclear fission and was passed over for the Nobel Prize that went to her male collaborator. Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin discovered that stars are mostly made of hydrogen and helium — a finding that revolutionized astronomy — and was initially told by her (male) supervisor that she must be wrong.

These women were brilliant. They were rigorous. They were right. And the history of science is full of them.

It is also worth noting that in many non-Western cultures and historical periods, women held prominent places in scientific and medical knowledge. This is a global history, and it belongs to all of us.

Today, women like Jennifer Doudna (co-inventor of CRISPR gene editing), Fabiola Gianotti (Director General of CERN), and Gitanjali Rao (young inventor and Time's first Kid of the Year) are rewriting what a scientist looks like.

You are not an exception to science. You are part of its future.

Some practical things to remember when you encounter the stereotype:
- If someone says *girls are bad at math*, remember: math ability is not biological. It is built through practice, encouragement, and good teaching.
- If you look around a classroom and you're the only girl interested in a STEM topic, that is a problem with the classroom — not with you.
- If you don't see women who look like you in STEM, that is an invitation — not a sign to leave.

---

### Block 3 — How to Use This Book

This book is organized into five parts, each focused on a different area of STEM. You do not need to read it from start to finish. You do not need to do every experiment. You do not need to complete any of them perfectly.

Here is how we suggest using it:

**Browse first.** Flip through the book and see what catches your attention. You might find yourself drawn to the engineering challenges in Part 3, or to the environmental projects in Part 5, or to the coding ideas in Part 4. Start where your curiosity pulls you.

**Choose one thing.** Pick one experiment or project to try. Just one. Don't overwhelm yourself. Read through it, gather your materials, and try it. See what happens.

**Write things down.** Get a notebook — it doesn't need to be anything fancy — and use it as a science journal. Write down what you expected to happen, what actually happened, and any questions the experiment raised. Scientists call this keeping a lab notebook. It is one of the most important habits in science.

**Don't skip the failures.** If something doesn't work, write that down too. Try to figure out why. Could your materials have been different? Did you miss a step? Was your hypothesis wrong? Investigating a failed experiment is often more educational than a successful one.

**Invite someone.** Science is more fun with company. Try the experiments with a friend, a sibling, a parent, or a teacher. Explain what you're doing. Teaching something is one of the best ways to understand it more deeply.

**Come back.** You don't have to do everything at once. This book is meant to be picked up again and again, over weeks and months and years. Some of the ideas here might seem too hard right now and feel just right in six months.

There is no grade at the end of this book. There is no test, no deadline, no right or wrong way to use it. There is only curiosity, and what happens when you follow it.

Let's begin.

# WOMEN WHO CHANGED THE WORLD THROUGH STEM

*Six extraordinary women. Six lives of curiosity, courage, and discovery.*

---

### Marie Curie (1867–1934)
**Physicist and Chemist | Poland and France**

Marie Curie is perhaps the most famous woman in the history of science — and for very good reason. Born Maria Skłodowska in Warsaw, Poland, she grew up in a country under foreign rule where women were barred from attending university. Rather than accept this, she made a deal with her sister: each would support the other's education in turns. Marie worked as a governess for years, sending money to her sister in Paris, before finally making the journey herself.

In Paris, she studied physics and mathematics at the Sorbonne, often in poverty, sometimes so cold in her rented room that the water in her washbasin froze overnight. She graduated first in her class. She met and married Pierre Curie, and together they began researching the mysterious rays emitted by uranium — a phenomenon she herself named *radioactivity*.

Her discoveries were staggering. She identified two new elements — polonium (named for her home country) and radium — and proved that radioactivity was an atomic property, not a chemical reaction. She won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1903 (the first woman ever to receive one, shared with Pierre and Henri Becquerel), and the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1911 — becoming the first person in history, male or female, to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.

What makes Curie an inspiration is not just her intelligence, but her persistence. She worked in a shed. She was denied positions because of her gender. Her discoveries were sometimes met with skepticism. She kept going anyway. And when World War I broke out, she drove mobile X-ray units to the front lines, saving soldiers' lives.

*What she shows us:* Obstacles are not verdicts. They are problems to be solved.

---

### Ada Lovelace (1815–1852)
**Mathematician | England**

Ada Lovelace lived in the 19th century, more than a hundred years before computers existed — and yet she is widely regarded as the world's first computer programmer.

Ada was the daughter of the poet Lord Byron, though she never knew him. Her mother, determined that Ada would not inherit her father's wild temperament, gave her an unusual-for-girls education heavily focused on mathematics. Ada showed an extraordinary gift, and at seventeen she met Charles Babbage, a mathematician who was designing a theoretical calculating machine he called the Analytical Engine.

Ada understood Babbage's machine — possibly better than Babbage himself did. When she translated an Italian article about the Analytical Engine into English, she added her own notes, which were three times longer than the original article. Those notes included what is now recognized as the first algorithm designed to be processed by a machine — making her the first person in history to describe what we now call software.

She also had a breathtaking vision of what computing could eventually become. She wrote that the Analytical Engine might one day compose music, produce graphics, and be used for scientific purposes — predictions that proved remarkably accurate, though it would take another century for anyone to build what she had imagined.

Ada died at just 36, never seeing her vision realized. But the programming language ADA, developed by the U.S. Department of Defense, was named in her honor. And every time you use software — every app, every website, every game — you are living in the world she first imagined.

*What she shows us:* Imagination and mathematics are not separate. The greatest thinking happens when they work together.

---

### Katherine Johnson (1918–2020)
**Mathematician | United States**

Katherine Johnson calculated the flight trajectories for some of the most important moments in space exploration history — including Alan Shepard's first American trip to space, John Glenn's orbit of Earth, and the Apollo 11 mission that landed humans on the moon.

She did this at a time when Black women in America were being told, by law and by custom, that many spaces were not for them. She worked at NASA (then called NACA) as a "human computer" — a term used for the teams of women, many of them Black, who performed the complex mathematical calculations that electronic computers were not yet trusted to do reliably.

John Glenn, one of the most celebrated astronauts in NASA history, famously refused to fly his orbital mission until Katherine Johnson personally verified the numbers that the electronic computer had produced. He trusted her mathematical precision over the machine. That trust was well placed. Glenn's mission succeeded.

Katherine Johnson worked at NASA for 35 years, authoring or co-authoring 26 scientific papers. In 2015, President Barack Obama awarded her the Presidential Medal of Freedom. She was portrayed in the 2016 film *Hidden Figures*, which finally brought her story to a wide audience — though she had been performing this extraordinary work, largely unsung, for decades.

She once said: *"I felt most proud of being accurate."* In a field where being off by a fraction of a degree could mean the difference between a spacecraft reaching the moon or disappearing into space, accuracy was everything. And she had it.

*What she shows us:* Precision is a superpower. And doing your job excellently, even when the world isn't watching, matters.

---

### Rosalind Franklin (1920–1958)
**Chemist and X-ray Crystallographer | England**

Rosalind Franklin is one of the most important — and most unjustly overlooked — figures in the history of biology. Using a technique called X-ray crystallography, she produced an image known as Photo 51: an X-ray diffraction photograph of DNA that is widely regarded as one of the most important images in the history of science.

That photograph revealed the helical structure of DNA — the double helix shape that carries the genetic information of all living things. When Watson and Crick announced their model of DNA in 1953, it was based significantly on Photo 51 and data from Franklin's work — data that was shared with them without her knowledge or permission. When Watson and Crick, along with Franklin's supervisor Maurice Wilkins, won the Nobel Prize in 1962, Franklin had already died of ovarian cancer at age 37. Nobel Prizes are not awarded posthumously, and her contribution was barely acknowledged in the speeches.

Franklin's story is one of the starkest examples of how women's scientific contributions have been erased and uncredited throughout history. But her work stands apart from how it was treated. She was a meticulous, brilliant experimentalist whose techniques advanced the understanding of molecular structure far beyond DNA — she also made major contributions to the study of viruses, including tobacco mosaic virus and polio.

*What she shows us:* The quality of your work matters, independently of whether you get credit for it. History has a way of finding the truth — and Rosalind Franklin's name is now permanently part of it.

---

### Mae Jemison (1956–present)
**Engineer, Physician, and Astronaut | United States**

On September 12, 1992, Mae Jemison became the first Black woman in history to travel to space. She flew aboard the Space Shuttle Endeavour as a mission specialist, conducting experiments in bone cell research and human adaptation to weightlessness. She holds the distinction of having worn two professional hats that most people would consider extraordinary on their own: she is both a medical doctor and an engineer.

Jemison grew up in Chicago and was interested in science from early childhood. She earned a chemical engineering degree from Stanford University and a medical degree from Cornell, served in the Peace Corps as a medical officer in Sierra Leone and Liberia, and then applied to NASA's astronaut program. She was selected from more than 2,000 applicants.

After leaving NASA, Jemison founded several companies focused on education and technology, including a science camp for students that she runs to this day. She also appeared in an episode of *Star Trek: The Next Generation* — a detail that reveals something important about her: she is, in the best sense, a dreamer. She believes in imagining the future we want to build, and then building it.

She has said: *"Never be limited by other people's limited imaginations."* This is not a platitude from her — it is literally how she has lived her life.

*What she shows us:* You can be many things at once. The categories that others put you in are not the edges of what you can become.

---

### Tu Youyou (1930–present)
**Pharmaceutical Chemist | China**

In the 1960s and 1970s, malaria — a parasitic disease spread by mosquitoes — was killing hundreds of thousands of people every year, and the parasites causing it were becoming resistant to existing drugs. The Chinese government launched a secret research initiative called Project 523 to find a new treatment. Tu Youyou was the lead scientist.

Working with a team and drawing on centuries of traditional Chinese medical texts, Tu screened hundreds of herbal compounds. She found a promising substance called artemisinin in sweet wormwood — a plant that had been used in Chinese medicine for over 1,500 years. But extracting it was difficult; early results were inconsistent. Tu returned to the ancient texts and found a 4th-century account suggesting that the plant should be prepared with cold water, not heat. She adapted her extraction method — and it worked.

Artemisinin is now the foundation of the most effective antimalarial treatments in the world. It has saved millions of lives, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. In 2015, Tu Youyou became the first Chinese woman to win the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine — at the age of 84, recognition that had taken decades to arrive.

Her work is a beautiful example of how traditional knowledge and modern science can work together: the ancient wisdom pointed the way; the modern method confirmed, refined, and scaled it.

*What she shows us:* Knowledge from unexpected places — including the past — can lead to the most important discoveries. Stay curious about everything.

# WHAT WE LEARN FROM WOMEN IN STEM

---

The six women you just met came from different countries, different centuries, and different fields of science. They faced different obstacles — poverty, racism, sexism, illness, erasure. And yet, looking across their lives, certain qualities appear again and again. These are the qualities that carried them through.

---

## 1. Curiosity — The Starting Engine

Every one of these women was, at her core, deeply curious. Marie Curie was fascinated by the invisible rays uranium produced and couldn't stop asking what they were. Ada Lovelace met a calculating machine and immediately began imagining what else it could do. Tu Youyou read a 1,500-year-old text and asked whether an ancient remedy might hold a modern answer.

Curiosity is not a character trait you either have or don't have. It is a habit you can practice. When you encounter something you don't understand, you have two choices: let it pass, or pause and ask *why*. The scientists we admire chose to pause.

In your STEM journey, curiosity is your most important tool. More important than any piece of equipment, any textbook, or any grade. If you leave every experiment with at least one new question — something you noticed, something that confused you, something you want to understand better — then you are doing science.

---

## 2. Persistence — Staying When It Gets Hard

None of the breakthroughs described in the previous pages came easily. Marie Curie ran hundreds of experiments to isolate radium. Katherine Johnson recalculated trajectories until they were exactly right. Tu Youyou tried extraction after extraction before finding the one that worked.

In between the successes, there were setbacks. There were failed experiments, rejected applications, misread data, and — perhaps most painfully — moments when the people around them doubted whether they belonged at all.

Persistence is not the same as stubbornness. Persistence means staying committed to your question even when you need to change your approach. It means learning from what went wrong and trying again, with new information. It means choosing to see an obstacle as a problem to solve rather than a reason to stop.

This book will give you fifty opportunities to practice persistence. Some experiments will work beautifully on the first try. Others might take three or four attempts. When something doesn't work, don't close the book. Ask yourself: *What did I observe? What might have gone differently? What would I change?* Then try again.

---

## 3. Courage — Moving Forward Anyway

It takes courage to walk into a space where you're not sure you're welcome. It takes courage to ask a question when you're afraid people will think it's obvious. It takes courage to defend a finding when others tell you you're wrong. It takes courage to apply for a job, a program, or a competition when you look around and don't see many people who look like you.

Every woman in the previous pages demonstrated this kind of courage. Katherine Johnson sat down at tables where Black women were not expected to sit. Rosalind Franklin worked in a field dominated by men who often underestimated her. Mae Jemison applied to astronaut training when she was told, implicitly and sometimes explicitly, that her kind of person wasn't what NASA was looking for.

Courage, in STEM, doesn't always look dramatic. Sometimes it is as simple as raising your hand in class when you have a question. Or asking your teacher to explain something again. Or writing down a hypothesis you're not sure is right and testing it anyway.

You don't have to feel brave to act bravely. You just have to act.

---

## 4. Creativity — Seeing What Others Don't See

Ada Lovelace saw a calculating machine and imagined music. Tu Youyou read a medieval medical text and saw a malaria cure. Katherine Johnson saw numbers and saw a path to the moon.

STEM is one of the most creative disciplines there is — a fact that is not always communicated well in school. But the heart of science is imagination: the ability to look at what exists and ask, *What if it worked differently? What if I combined these two things? What if this over here is connected to that over there?*

Creativity in STEM is not about being "arty." It is about being willing to think outside the expected solution, to combine ideas from different fields, to look at an old problem from a new angle. The best scientific breakthroughs almost always involve someone seeing a connection that no one else had noticed.

As you work through the experiments in this book, practice asking creative questions. *What would happen if I changed the temperature? What would happen if I used more? What would happen if I did it backwards?* Let your imagination into the laboratory. It belongs there.

---

## 5. Identity — Belonging Without Changing

Perhaps the most important lesson of all: none of the women in the previous pages became scientists by becoming someone else. Marie Curie brought her Polish identity into her work, naming a new element after her home country. Mae Jemison moved between medicine, engineering, and space — refusing to be just one thing. Tu Youyou bridged two worlds of knowledge that others saw as separate.

Your identity — your culture, your background, your way of seeing the world, the specific questions your life has made you ask — is not an obstacle to your STEM career. It is an asset. Diverse perspectives produce better science. The questions you ask, because of who you are and where you come from, may be exactly the questions the field needs.

**You do not have to look like everyone else to belong in STEM.**

You do not have to sound like everyone else, think like everyone else, or come from the same places everyone else comes from. The door is open. It has always been open, even when people tried to close it. Walk through it.

# PART 1

# SCIENCE AT HOME

---

*Science doesn't begin in a laboratory. It begins the moment you look at something familiar and ask a question you've never asked before.*

*Your kitchen, your garden, your bathroom shelf, your window sill — all of these are laboratories waiting to happen. The baking soda in your cupboard, the sunlight coming through your window, the lemon sitting in your fruit bowl: these are not just household objects. They are invitations.*

*The ten experiments in Part 1 are designed to help you discover science in the places you already know. They use materials you likely have at home or can find easily at a market. They don't require expensive equipment. They do require your curiosity, your willingness to try, and your attention.*

*Science at home is not "baby science." Some of the most important principles in physics, chemistry, and biology can be demonstrated with a glass of water, a handful of salt, or a jar and a candle. When you understand the science behind everyday phenomena, the world opens up in a completely new way. You start to see the physics in a soap bubble, the chemistry in a cup of tea, the biology in the bread you had for breakfast.*

*Pay attention. Ask questions. Write things down.*

*The laboratory is already yours.*
